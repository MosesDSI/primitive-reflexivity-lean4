I picked up `OrderObstruction.lean` where the technical paper left it: `QS`, the a+b√2 model, had multiplication and an order but no addition, negation, or inverse — a scaffold sized to exactly one theorem. Section 9.3 of the paper lays out a six-step roadmap to turn it into a certified ordered field; I did Steps 1 and 2.

The first thing worth recording isn't a proof, it's an environment check. Both the paper and the task that pointed me at it say Lean 4.34.1. This machine doesn't have that toolchain — it has 4.33.1 (what the rest of this repo is actually pinned to) and a 4.34.0-rc2 that nothing here uses. I checked `lean-toolchain` and `lakefile.toml` rather than assuming the paper's stated version was current, and built against 4.33.1 instead. Small thing, but "the paper says X" and "X is what's actually installed" turned out to be two different claims, and only one of them was true.

The additive instances and their six laws (`add_assoc`, `add_comm`, `zero_add`, `add_zero`, `add_left_neg`, `sub_eq_add_neg`) were mechanical — componentwise on the two `Rat` fields, closed by the same `show`-then-`grind` pattern the file already uses for multiplication. Nothing interesting happened there, which is itself a small confirmation that the file's existing proof style generalizes cleanly.

The inverse was where I had to be careful. The roadmap says the norm a²−2b² is guaranteed nonzero for nonzero elements by "Proposition 1.1" — meaning `rat_no_sqrt_two`, already sitting in the file. Proving that meant, for the `x.b ≠ 0` branch, actually constructing `x.a/x.b` as a witness and showing `(x.a/x.b)*(x.a/x.b) = 2`, then handing it straight to `rat_no_sqrt_two` for the contradiction. That's a genuine division argument, and this file has no `field_simp`, no `ring`, none of Mathlib's automation for clearing denominators — just `Rat.div_mul_cancel` and `Rat.mul_div_cancel`, used exactly the way `step_below`/`step_above` already used them two hundred lines up. I didn't reach for a fancier tool; I used the one the file already trusted.

`mul_inv_cancel` needed the same discipline twice over — two components, each requiring the same "multiply through by the nonzero norm, reduce to `Rat.mul_eq_zero`" argument. I wrote it once, saw it worked, and repeated the shape rather than trying to find a shorter combined proof; a proof that reads as the same move twice is easier to trust than a clever one that reads as new each time.

One real mistake, caught by the kernel and not by me: my first pass at the `x.b = 0` case of `norm_eq_zero_iff` left the goal `{a:=0,b:=0} = 0` unsolved after `rw [ha, hb]`, because `rw` closes by `rfl` syntactically and `(0 : QS)` doesn't auto-unfold to `⟨0,0⟩` for that check. I hadn't predicted that failure; the compiler told me. Fixed by stating the target as the literal `⟨0, 0⟩` before the rewrite instead of the instance-wrapped `0`.

Everything — the six additive laws, `norm_eq_zero_iff`, `mul_inv_cancel` — checks out at `[propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound]`, the same trust base the rest of `QS` already carries. No `sorry`, no `native_decide`, no new axioms. I also ran the file through `leanchecker` independently of the elaborator that produced it (per the paper's own Appendix A method), and ran a negative control first — pointed `leanchecker` at a `LEAN_PATH` that couldn't find the compiled module, confirmed it actually fails in that case — before trusting the clean exit on the real run. A checker that can't fail isn't checking anything.

What I didn't do: wire this file into the `lake` build. It still lives standalone, checked by raw `lean`, same as the paper describes it. The task left that as optional, and Steps 1–2 didn't need it, so I left it — but it's still true, and worth saying plainly rather than letting "verified" quietly expand to mean "integrated."
